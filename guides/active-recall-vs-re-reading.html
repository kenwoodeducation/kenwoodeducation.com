Why Re-Reading Notes Doesn't Work (And What Does)

Most students revise by reading through their notes, highlighting textbooks, and watching videos on repeat. It feels productive. The research is fairly clear that it barely works.

The problem with re-reading

Re-reading creates a feeling called fluency. The material looks familiar, the words seem recognisable, and it's easy to assume you know it. But familiarity isn't the same as being able to reproduce an answer under exam conditions, and that's the distinction exams test.

A student who has read through their Chemistry notes three times can feel confident going into a test. But when asked to write out the steps of a reaction from memory, the same student often finds the knowledge isn't as accessible as it seemed. They could recognise the answer if they saw it. They can't produce it when they need to.

This matters because GCSE and A-level exams are almost entirely production tasks, not recognition tasks. There's no multiple choice to rely on. Everything comes from memory, under time pressure, without notes.

What the research actually says

Research on revision techniques is more conclusive than most people realise. Roediger and Karpicke (2006) compared students who re-read material with students who tested themselves on it, using the same study time. After one week, students using active recall retained 80% of material. Students who re-read retained 36%.

That's not a marginal difference. It's the same amount of time producing roughly double the retention, just by changing what the student did with that time.

A comprehensive review of ten popular study techniques by Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated practice testing as "high utility," one of only two methods to earn that rating out of ten. Re-reading and highlighting were both rated "low utility."

The gap between what feels effective and what works

The techniques students use most (re-reading, highlighting, summarising) are consistently rated lowest for long-term retention. The techniques rated highest (practice testing, spaced repetition) are the ones most students barely use. The mismatch is almost entirely explained by the fact that passive methods feel more comfortable and less effortful, which is exactly why they don't work as well.

What active recall actually looks like

Active recall means testing yourself on material rather than reviewing it. Every time a student forces their brain to retrieve information from memory, the neural pathways that store that information are strengthened. The effort of retrieval is the mechanism. That's why it works, and why re-reading doesn't replicate it.

Passive techniqueActive alternative
Re-reading notesClose the notes, write down everything you can remember about the topic (blurting)
Highlighting a textbookCover the page and try to recall the key points before looking
Watching a summary videoAfter watching, pause and explain the concept out loud from memory
Reading through flashcardsCover the answer, retrieve it from memory, then check
Reading a worked exampleAttempt the problem yourself before looking at the solution

The common thread is forcing retrieval. A student who blurts out everything they remember about the causes of World War One, then checks their notes to see what they missed, is doing something fundamentally different from a student who reads through the same notes and feels satisfied that it all looks familiar.

Past papers are the most powerful version

Past papers under timed conditions are the most direct form of active recall available for GCSE and A-level students. They replicate the exact conditions of the exam, force retrieval across multiple topics simultaneously, and reveal with precision which areas of knowledge are genuinely solid and which only feel familiar. Looking at the mark scheme afterward, and understanding exactly why marks were lost, is at least as valuable as the attempt itself.

Most students do past papers too late, under-prepared and under pressure. The more useful approach is to use them throughout the revision period as diagnostics, not just in the final weeks as practice runs.

Where a tutor makes a difference

A good tutor naturally forces active recall in every session. They ask questions, require a student to explain concepts out loud, work through problems without the student looking at notes first, and identify exactly where retrieval fails rather than where recognition fails. That's a genuinely different experience from self-study, where it's easy to convince yourself you know something simply because the answer looks familiar when you read it.

The value isn't just in the subject knowledge a tutor brings. It's in the fact that being asked "explain this to me" is active recall, in a way that reading the same content for a fourth time isn't. A session with a good tutor is, almost by definition, an active recall session.

Frequently asked questions

What is active recall?
Testing yourself on material rather than reviewing it. Every time you try to pull an answer from memory without looking at your notes, you are using active recall. Re-reading puts information in front of your eyes; active recall forces your brain to retrieve it.

Is re-reading notes bad for revision?
Not harmful, but significantly less effective than active methods. Roediger and Karpicke (2006) found 80% retention after one week with active recall versus 36% with re-reading. Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated re-reading "low utility" and practice testing "high utility."

What are some active recall revision techniques?
Flashcards (covering the answer and retrieving from memory), blurting (writing down everything you can remember before checking notes), past papers under timed conditions, and explaining a topic out loud as if teaching someone else. All force retrieval rather than re-exposure.

How does a tutor help with active recall?
A good tutor asks questions, requires explanation out loud, and identifies exactly where retrieval fails. This is harder to replicate in self-study, where a student can mistake recognition for knowledge.

Sources: Roediger, H.L. & Karpicke, J.D. (2006), "Test-Enhanced Learning," Psychological Science, 17(3), 249–255. Dunlosky, J. et al. (2013), "Improving Students' Learning With Effective Study Techniques," Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 14(1), 4–58.
