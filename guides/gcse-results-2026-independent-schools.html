GCSE Results 2026: Independent Schools In and Around North London

A look at how common top grades have become at some of the selective independent schools North London families consider, where the figures come from, and what that actually means for A-level planning.

The numbers

All figures below are the schools' own published 2026 results (Mill Hill's figures are provisional, ahead of final reviews of marking).

School Grade 9 Grade 8-9
NLCS 79.4% 94.6%
Highgate School 61.7% 86.6%
South Hampstead High School 62% 84%
UCS 58% 84%
Habs Boys 56.2% 80.1%
Habs Girls 45% 73%
Channing School 37.2% 64.8%
Mill Hill School 25% 51%

For context, the national average grade-9 rate across England sits at a small fraction of these figures, in the low single digits in most years. At several of the schools above, grade 9, the single highest mark available, is now one of the most commonly awarded grades, not a rare exception.

Why this varies between schools

The differences between schools on this list largely reflect differences in entry selectivity and cohort size, not a simple ranking of teaching quality. Schools with highly competitive entry processes at 11+ or 7+ tend to draw from a narrower, higher-scoring applicant pool, which shows up in GCSE outcomes years later. A school lower on this list is still performing exceptionally by national standards, even where its grade-9 rate sits well below the highest figures here.

Cohort size and subject mix also play a role. A school with a larger number of IGCSE entries, for example, can see its overall percentages shift slightly compared to a school running mostly standard GCSEs, since grading conventions between the two aren't always identical. None of this changes the underlying picture: every school on this list is performing well above the national average, the differences between them are a matter of degree, not category.

How these figures compare to the national picture

Nationally, the proportion of GCSE entries awarded a grade 9 typically sits in the low single digits in any given year, a figure that's remained broadly stable since the 9-1 scale was fully rolled out. Against that backdrop, even the lowest grade-9 rate on this list, Mill Hill's provisional 25%, is a multiple of the national average. At the top end, NLCS's 79.4% means that at that particular school, achieving the highest possible grade is closer to the norm than the exception.

This matters for how families read a results table like this one. A grade that would stand out as exceptional against the national average can be a mid-cohort result at some of the schools listed here, which is exactly why understanding a school's own grade distribution, not just the national one, matters when planning next steps.

What this means in practice

When top grades are this common, the real question for a family isn't "did they pass." It's how a 7, 8 or 9 shapes which subjects a student can continue at A-level, and how a school internally distinguishes between students who are, on paper, all performing at a similar level. General guidance suggests a grade 6 is usually enough to continue a subject, but it's always worth confirming the specific requirement directly with the school, particularly at a highly selective cohort where a 6 can sit toward the lower end.

What to do with this

  • Ask the school what grade they'd recommend for each specific subject at A-level, not just the general national guidance.
  • Check where a grade sits within your child's own year group, not just against national averages. A grade that looks strong nationally can sit toward the lower end of a highly selective cohort.
